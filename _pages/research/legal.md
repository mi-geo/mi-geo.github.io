## Background
My research on the uneven distribution of legal resources in the United States emerged from a broader concern with spatial inequality and access to justice. Across much of the rural South and Midwest, defendants often face a shortage of qualified defense attorneys, resulting in prolonged case processing times and unequal representation. This condition—commonly referred to as a legal desert—reflects deeper structural imbalances in how professional and institutional resources are distributed across space.

As research deepening, I gradually developed new methods to explore the features and patterns of the evolvment of legal resources. 

#### Education Analysis:
Attorneys are trained in diverse law schools across the country. Unlike other professions, I observed that lawyers tend to establish their practices near the locations of their law schools. My initial research sought to explore how legal education influences the geographic distribution of legal resources. However, this line of inquiry was not pursued further due to limited data on the hometown origins of law students, which constrained the ability to trace their professional mobility after graduation.

#### Network features
My analysis shows that legal resources have become increasingly concentrated around a few courts and clusters of legal professionals. Over time, this centralization has created pockets of abundance surrounded by large areas of scarcity, highlighting how uneven access to legal representation can reinforce broader social and geographic inequalities.
This research is on-going.
